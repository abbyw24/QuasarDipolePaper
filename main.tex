\begin{abstract}\noindent
    There is a reported discrepancy in amplitude between the dipole measured in the cosmic microwave background and the dipole measured in the distribution of radio and infrared selected quasars.
    These dipoles should be consistent if they are both caused primarily by our peculiar motion with respect to the CMB rest frame.
    Here we investigate the kinematic dipole in the sky distribution of two all-sky quasar catalogs, \quaia and \catwise, under two different frameworks.
    In the first, we adopt a Bayesian forward-modeling approach to generate all-sky mock density maps for each catalog and employ map-level inference to measure the dipole amplitude along the CMB-measured direction.

Our peculiar velocity relative to the rest frame of the Universe generates a dipole in the signals we measure across the sky.
The CMB exhibits a temperature dipole, taken to be purely kinematic in origin and used to infer a heliocentric velocity of
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:CMB_velocity}
    v_0 = (369.825\pm 0.070)\,\mathrm{km\, s}^{-1}\ \mathrm{towards}\ (l,b) = (264.021\pm0.009^\circ, 48.253\pm0.004^\circ)
\end{equation}
\citep{planck_collaboration_planck_2020}.
This velocity is a net vector sum of three relative motions: the Sun around the Milky Way, the Milky Way's motion within the Local Group, and the Local Group's motion relative to the CMB.
The dipole ($\ell=1$) is the dominant CMB anisotropy with fluctuation amplitude $\mathcal{D}\sim 10^{-3}$.
This Doppler boost of the CMB monopole is so well-measured by Planck that its expected modulations and aberrations of the temperature fluctuations have been detected in higher modes of the CMB spectrum \citep{planck_collaboration_planck_2014}.

The CMB and LSS rest frames should converge under the standard $\Lambda$CDM model; \ksf{i would first introduce the LSS dipole, and then say this bit about that they should be the same} the kinematic dipole anisotropy in the number count of LSS tracers should align with the CMB temperature dipole in direction, with its amplitude modified by a prefactor that depends on the tracer sample.
Two effects contribute to the total dipole: Doppler boosting causes sources to appear brighter in the direction of our motion, and relativistic aberration causes sources to appear closer together in the direction of our motion.
The predominant approximation comes from \ksf{this phrase is a bit unclear, is there better wording?} \citet{ellis_expected_1984} and gives the expected kinematic dipole in a flux-limited sample of discrete sources as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ellisbaldwin}
    \vec{\mathcal{D}} \simeq \left[2+x(1+\alpha)\right]\vobs/c
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the number-count slope of the sources at the magnitude limit of the sample, and $\alpha$ is the effective spectral index of the sources, defined by the assumed power law distribution of the source flux density.
The expected dipole generates a $\sim$$0.5\%$ variation in the mean tracer density across the sky.

Previous analyses found a tension between the dipole measured in the LSS and the CMB expectation, which would break the assumption of cosmic isotropy.
Several of the initial analyses were performed on radio source counts from the 1.4 GHz NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS; \citealt{condon_nrao_1998}).
Early work, such as \citet{blake_detection_2002}, found this matter dipole to be broadly consistent with the expectation from the CMB, though with large uncertainties.

\quaia, a more recent all-sky spectroscopic quasar sample constructed from the space-based \gaia and \wise surveys, offers a new opportunity to measure the LSS dipole \citep{storey-fisher_quaia_2024}.
The catalog is not fully independent from the S21 sample, since both rely on infrared observations from \textit{WISE}.
Nevertheless, \quaia involves different observations, source selection, and systematics, and therefore can serve as a check on the dipole measured in S21.
\citet{mittal_cosmic_2023} conducted a Bayesian analysis of the \quaia $G\le20.0$ sample and found its dipole to be consistent with the expectation in both direction and amplitude.
By contrast, the \quaia dipole measured by \citet{singal_cosmic_2024} was in the expected direction but 3-4 times larger in amplitude.
With a significantly high dipole amplitude reported by several different analyses, across independent samples selected at multiple wavelengths, the question arises whether the dipole tension could indicate a breakdown of the standard cosmological model or whether it can be explained by a systematic effect in the data or analysis techniques.

Some recent work has focused on possible [issues with/systematic effects in] previous dipole analyses.
One common approach is to decompose the source density sky map into spherical harmonic modes, with the amplitude of the dipole given by the $\ell=1$ spherical harmonic coefficients.
S21 uses this technique to measure the \catwise dipole via a linear least-squares fit.
However, \citet{abghari_reassessment_2024} points out that mode-coupling effects induced by the cut sky can cause power in higher angular modes to leak into the dipole ($\ell=1$) mode, inflating the dipole signal.
Other studies have reassessed the Ellis-Baldwin dipole approximation, claiming that it may be inaccurate since it ignores the redshift evolution of the sources in the sample \citep{dalang_kinematic_2022, guandalin_theoretical_2022}.
However, \citet{von_hausegger_expected_2024} argues that the approximation is robust to such effects.

Another consideration is the contribution of local clustering and shot noise to the measured dipole.
\citet{cheng_is_2023} points out that our motion relative to the CMB rest frame is not the only dipole signal: in discrete samples, clustering of local sources, as well as the Poisson fluctuations in the source counts---shot noise---also contribute to the $\ell=1$ mode of the power spectrum.
Thus the total dipole in a sample is the sum of the kinematic dipole, the clustering dipole, and the shot-noise dipole, the latter two of which can be combined into a single ``local-source" term.
Many analyses including S21 assume the local-source term to be negligible compared to the kinematic dipole; that is, that the total dipole measured in a sample can be approximated as the kinematic dipole.
\citet{cheng_is_2023} applied their formalism to NVSS sources and found that on the contrary, the local-source dipole is comparable in amplitude to the kinematic dipole and therefore cannot be neglected when comparing the total measured dipole to the expected kinematic dipole.
However, they note that \citet{dam_testing_2022} did carefully account for the local-source dipole and still found a $5.7\sigma$ discrepancy between the measured kinematic dipole in \catwise and the CMB dipole expectation, so the local-source term alone may not resolve the dipole tension.
In addition, \citet{singal_cosmic_2024} reanalyzed the NVSS sample and argue that the local-source term cannot account for the large observed dipole; \cite{von_hausegger_clustering_2025} also found the effect of the clustering dipole to be marginal for \catwise.

In this work, we measure the number-count dipole in the \quaia and S21 samples and compare each to their expectation: the direction of the CMB dipole with amplitude given by eq. \ref{eq:ellisbaldwin}.
We adopt two different approaches:
First, we use the ``standard'' technique employed by S21 and several other works, which involves a linear least-squares fit to three orthogonal dipole templates.
We confirm the S21 result with this method in the \catwise catalog.
We also employ a new forward-modeling approach to infer both the amplitude of the dipole in the expected direction and the overall level of excess angular power at multipoles $\ell\le 8$.
We generate mock all-sky quasar density maps with the same selection function as applied to the real data and perform map-level inference via an Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) scheme to estimate the parameter posteriors.
This approach is designed to avoid potential mode-coupling effects, and it directly infers the kinematic dipole amplitude along the CMB dipole direction, so the shot-noise contribution to the dipole is taken into account separately.

\subsection{The Quaia Quasar Sample}

\subsection{Masks and Selection Functions}
\label{sec:masks_and_selfuncs}

We inherit the masking procedure used in S21 and apply it to both the \quaia and \catwise samples.
To reduce contamination from stars and dust, we mask the galactic plane below $\vert b\vert = 30^\circ$.
To reduce contamination from spurious sources, we also mask several smaller regions: the MCs, bright galaxies from 2MASS, bright artifacts from \wise, and other areas of low completeness.
Note that if any sources in a healpixel are masked, the entire healpixel gets masked.
The $\nside=64$ binary mask is applied to the data sample before any dipole analyses.

In addition to the binary mask, we model each sample's selection function, which gives the completeness in each pixel relative to the predicted number of quasars that would be observed in the absence of any selection effects.
To model the selection function, \cite{storey-fisher_quaia_2024} uses a Gaussian process to fit Healpix maps ($\nside=64$) of relevant systematics templates to the source counts in each healpixel.
The systematics templates for \quaia are dust extinction, \gaia and \unwise source density, and those missions' scanning laws.
In this work, we generated an updated selection function with the addition of a zodiacal light template.
We apply the same procedure to \catwise, using the \unwise source density, \unwise scanning law ($M_{10}$), dust extinction, and zodiacal light as the templates.
To correct the observed counts by the selection function, we simply divide the counts by the relative completeness in each pixel.

To construct the zodiacal light templates, we use the Astropy-affiliated package \texttt{ZodiPy} \citep{san_zodipy_2024}, which implements the \citet{kelsall_cobe_1998} model from DIRBE, modified with zodiacal emission data from \citet{planck_collaboration_planck_2014}.

We take the mean of these maps to create a single full-sky, time-averaged Healpix map.
For the wavelengths, \wise observes in bands W1 (3.4 $\mu$m) and W2 (4.6 $\mu$m).

\section{The number-count dipole}
\label{sec:dipole_theory}

\subsection{The Ellis-Baldwin approximation}
\label{sec:EB}
Under the kinematic interpretation of the dipole, both the CMB and matter dipole anisotropies are caused by our proper motion with respect to the CMB rest frame, that is, the frame in which the CMB is maximally isotropic.
Therefore the expected direction of the matter dipole is the same as the CMB dipole (eq. \ref{eq:CMB_velocity}).
The expected amplitude of the matter dipole depends on the velocity $\vobs$ of the observer and the properties of the sources in the sample.

eq. \ref{eq:ellisbaldwin} gives the expected dipole amplitude for a flux-limited sample.
The prefactor in front of our peculiar velocity is the result of two special relativistic effects: there is an abberration effect causing sources in direction $\hat{v}_0$ to appear closer together, and there is a Doppler shift causing sources in direction $\hat{v}_0$ to appear brighter.
These effects result in additional dependencies on the slope of the number of sources at the magnitude limit of the sample as well as the effective spectral index of the sample.
As described in \citet{ellis_expected_1984}, both of these values come in at roughly order unity.

We calculate the number-count slope $x$ using
\begin{equation}
    x \equiv -\left.\frac{\dd\ln N(>S_\nu)}{\dd\ln S_\nu}\right|_{S_\mathrm{min}} ~,
\end{equation}
where $S_\nu$ is the flux density, $N(>S_\nu)$ is the number of sources above a given flux density limit, and $S_\mathrm{min}$ is the flux-density limit of the sample.
As we travel in direction $\hat v_0$, the Doppler shift causes sources in that direction whose flux at rest would be barely fainter than $S_\mathrm{min}$ to increase to brighter than $S_\mathrm{min}$ such that we observe them; we thus see more sources in the direction of our motion and fewer sources in the opposite direction, which results in a dipole in the source counts.
We calculate the slope $x$ with a linear fit to the counts in the two bins on either side of the counts at $S_\mathrm{min}$.
For \catwise, the number-count slope at the fiducial magnitude limit $\w 1=16.4$ is $x=1.75$.
For \quaia, the number-count slope at the fiducial magnitude limit $\g =20.0$ is $x=1.30$. 

To calculate $\alpha$, we assume that the flux density of each source roughly follows a power law, $S_\nu(\nu)\sim\nu^{-\alpha}$, which is a reasonable approximation for quasar spectra.
We can estimate $\alpha$ for any source with flux measurements at two or more frequencies.
If we have magnitudes of the source in two spectral bands, $m_1$ and $m_2$ in the AB system, from the definition of magnitude they are related to flux by
\begin{equation}
    (m_1-m_2)_\mathrm{AB}=-2.5\,(\log S_{\nu,m_1}-\log S_{\nu,m_2}) ~,
\end{equation}
where $S_{\nu,m_i}$ is the flux density (units of W m$^{-2}$ Hz$^{-1}$) in spectral band $m_i$.
Since magnitudes in the AB system are defined relative to a source with flat spectrum such that $\nu S_\nu=$ constant, $\alpha$ can be computed from the slope of the AB magnitudes:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = -\frac{\dd\log S_\nu}{\dd\log\nu} \approx -\frac{\log S_{\nu,m_1}-\log S_{\nu,m_2}}{\log\left(\nu_{m_1}/\nu_{m_2}\right)} = -\frac{1}{2.5}\frac{(m_1-m_2)_{\mathrm{AB}}}{\log\lambda_{m_2} -\log\lambda_{m_1}} ~,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the wavelength, $\lambda=\frac{c}{\nu}$.
Both \gaia and \wise report magnitudes in the Vega system, so we convert to AB magnitudes using the photometric zeropoints in each band (BP$-$RP and W1$-$W2).

\subsection{The dipole definition}
\label{sec:dipole_model}

The dipole anisotropy in the source counts in direction $\hat n=(\theta,\phi)$ \abby{Hogg: this isn't correct, right? with $\hat n$ being a 3-vector and $(\theta,\phi)$ a 2-vector on the sphere... how do I translate this?} on the sky can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}(\theta,\phi)=\vec{\mathcal{D}}\cdot \hat n = \sum_{j=1}^3 d_jD_j(\theta,\phi)
\end{equation}
where $\vec{\mathcal{D}}\equiv(d_1,d_2,d_3)$ is a 3-vector of amplitudes, and $D_j(\theta,\phi)$ are three orthogonal dipole templates, shown in Figure \ref{fig:dipole_templates}.
Under the assumption that the observed quasar distribution is isotropic but modified by kinematic effects, as predicted by the cosmological principle, the expected number of quasars at $(\theta,\phi)$ on the sky is the sum of the monopole and dipole contributions:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dipole_model}
    n'(\theta,\phi) = \bar n + \mathcal{D}(\theta,\phi)
\end{equation}
where $\bar n$ is the monopole, the mean number of quasars in the map.

Of course, the amplitude of the dipole (e.g. as given by eq. \ref{eq:ellisbaldwin}) is the usual norm of the vector,
\begin{equation}
    |\vec{\mathcal{D}}|=\sqrt{d_1^2 + d_2^2 + d_3^2} \, .
\end{equation}

\section{Dipole measurement}

\subsection{Linear least-squares fit}
\label{sec:standard_fit}

The most straightforward way to measure the dipole involves a linear least-squares fit to the three orthogonal dipole templates (Figure \ref{fig:dipole_templates}).
We use the model given by eq. \ref{eq:dipole_model}, so the four free parameters are the monopole and the amplitudes of the three dipole components:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\theta} = \begin{bmatrix}
           \bar n \\
           d_1 \\
           d_2 \\
           d_3
         \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

For some choice of $\vec\theta$, the total squared error for $N$ healpixels is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chisq}
    \chi^2 = \sum_i^{N} \frac{\left(n_i - n'(\theta_i,\phi_i)\right)^2}{\sigma_i^2}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the observed number of quasars in the $i$th healpixel, $n'(\theta_i,\phi_i)$ is the number of quasars predicted by the model evaluated at the location of the pixel on the sky $(\theta_i,\phi_i)$, and $\sigma_i^2$ is the variance, which we take to be the inverse of the completeness, the value of the selection function in the pixel.
The best-fit values of $\vec\theta$ are those which minimize $\chi^2$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:theta_lstsq}
    \vec\theta = \left[\bm{A}^T\,\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{A} \right]^{-1}\left[\bm{A}^T\,\bm{C}^{-1}\,\bm{Y}\right]
\end{equation}
where $\bm{A}$ is a $N\times 4$ design matrix containing ones in the first column and the orthogonal dipole templates $D_j(\theta_i,\phi_i)$ evaluated in each pixel on the sky in the last three columns, $\bm{Y}$ is the $N\times 1$ data vector of observed quasars $n_i$, and $\bm{C}^{-1}$ is the inverse covariance matrix.
S21 measures the dipole in the \catwisetwentytwenty sample (Figure \ref{fig:S21_map}) using the \texttt{healpy.fit\_dipole()} function, which performs this fit, assuming the identity matrix for $\bm{C}^{-1}$.

Importantly, though the spherical harmonics are orthogonal on the full sky, the cut sky induces mode coupling.
\citet{abghari_reassessment_2024} finds that significant leakage from $\ell >1$ into the $\ell=1$ mode occurs when roughly half of the sky is masked, as in the \quaia and \catwise samples.
In particular, the dominance of the quadrupole ($\ell=2$) in the galactic plane mask strongly couples the dipole ($\ell=1$) and octupole ($\ell=3$), leading to a large uncertainty in the measured dipole amplitude.

One approach to mitigate the mode coupling induced by the cut sky is the inclusion of a regularization parameter in the least-squares fit.
This parameter $\lambda$ penalizes large values of the parameters in $\vec\theta$.
Using ridge regularization, the parameter vector (eq. \ref{eq:theta_lstsq}) becomes
\begin{equation}
    \vec\theta = \left[\bm{A}^T\,\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{A} + \bm\Lambda\right]^{-1}\left[\bm{A}^T\,\bm{C}^{-1}\,\bm{Y}\right]
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\Lambda}=\lambda\bm{I}$, with $\bm{I}$ the identity matrix.
We explored regularization, but without a principled cross-validation scheme, there is no clear way to determine the appropriate choice for the value of the hyperparameter $\lambda$.
The potential mode-coupling effects present in the standard least-squares fitting method, combined with the uncertainty in the choice of regularization parameter $\lambda$, motivated the introduction of an alternative dipole measurement approach, presented below.

\subsection{Forward-modeling and inference approach}
\label{sec:FLI}

We use a forward-modeling approach to perform inference at the map level, learning posterior probability distributions over the dipole amplitude in the quasar catalogs.
In particular, we employ Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC), a likelihood-free parameter inference method, implemented using the \texttt{pyABC} package \citep{schalte_pyabc_2022}.
\texttt{pyABC} uses a sequential Monte-Carlo (SMC) scheme to iteratively update the posterior probability distribution.
ABC is particularly suited for complex, stochastic models for which it is difficult (or impossible) to write down the likelihood function.
The primary requirements for ABC-SMC are an executable forward-process model, a prior distribution for each parameter, a choice of distance metric, and a few hyperparameters.

With our model, we test a specific hypothesis motivated by previous claims in the literature: that the quasar dipole points roughly in the CMB dipole direction with a different amplitude than expected.
The model contains a single free parameter, the kinematic dipole amplitude $\dipamp$, and assumes that the quasar distribution is isotropic in all modes except the dipole, which points in the CMB dipole direction with some amplitude $\dipamp$.

Both models are evaluated on a healpixel grid.
The quasar density at sky position $(\theta,\phi)$ is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(\theta,\phi) = \left[1 + \mathcal{D}_\mathrm{k}(\theta,\phi)\right]\,\rho
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{D}_\mathrm{k}(\theta,\phi)$ the kinematic dipole contribution to the overdensity.
$\rho$ is the quasar base rate, the mean number of quasars in the unmasked pixels, which determines the level of shot noise in the mock map.
To determine the base rate for each catalog, we initially included it as a free parameter in the model.
We found the base rate to be so well-constrained, and independent of the other parameters, that we fixed it to this inferred value in the rest of our inferences.
See Table \ref{tab:} for the quasar base rates used for each sample. \abby{add this}

We tried testing a second model which allows additional anisotropy by including a second free parameter $\bar C$, the excess power at the largest angular scales.
Under this model, the quasar density is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(\theta,\phi) = \left[1 + \mathcal{D}_\mathrm{k}(\theta,\phi) + \gamma_{\bar C}(\theta, \phi)\right]\,\rho
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{\bar C}(\theta,\phi)$ is the excess angular power contribution to the overdensity at angle $(\theta,\phi)$ on the sky.
``Excess" power here means power in addition to shot noise; the model incorporates shot noise separately, so a quasar map with only dipole anisotropy and shot noise would be consistent with zero excess power.
The parameter $\bar C$ determined the amount of excess angular power at scales $1\le \ell\le 8$, assumed to be roughly flat in $C(\ell)$ space.
While this was a simple and particular model of excess power, we hoped that it would allow us to infer the contribution of potential higher-order modes that may be contaminating the dipole measurement.
After conducting several recovery tests with the ABC on mock quasar maps, however, the posterior distributions did not converge to the true level of excess power injected into the mock data.

The map $\lambda(\theta,\phi)$ returns the number of quasars observed in a healpixel centered at $(\theta,\phi)$ in the absence of shot noise and selection effects.
To incorporate these observational effects, we multiply the map by the selection function (Section \ref{sec:masks_and_selfuncs}) and draw the final quasar counts in each pixel from a Poisson distribution with expectation $\lambda(\theta,\phi)$.
Finally, the counts are converted to overdensities.
An example mock generated using this model is shown in Figure \ref{fig:example_mock}.

The total dipole in the map is the sum of the kinematic dipole, the shot noise dipole, and the ``excess power" dipole, which we define as the excess angular power $\bar C$ injected into the $\ell=1$ mode (transformed to dipole amplitude; see Section \ref{sec:D_to_C1}):
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\mathcal{D}}_\mathrm{tot}=\vec{\mathcal{D}}_\mathrm{k} + \vec{\mathcal{D}}_\mathrm{SN} +\vec{\mathcal{D}}_\gamma 
\end{equation}
Note that the total dipole might not (in fact, will not) point exactly in the same direction as the CMB dipole, since while $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\mathrm{k}$ is fixed, $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\mathrm{SN}$ and $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\gamma$ depend on stochastic Poisson fluctuations and the stochastic draw of the spherical harmonic coefficients respectively and can point in any direction.

To infer the free parameter $\dipamp$, the ABC calls the model many times, each time with the dipole amplitude sampled at random from the prior.
The goal is to accept mocks that look like the data and reject the others, keeping track of the values of the parameters used to generate the accepted mocks.
Quantitatively, for ABC, this means condensing each mock into some summary statistic, then comparing the summary statistics of the mock and the observed data via a chosen distance metric.

We generate the mock quasar maps at $\nside=64$, and the summary statistic is the overdensity map downgraded to $\nside=2$, which corresponds to a pixel size of $29.3\,\mathrm{deg}^2$, for faster convergence.
We ran inference tests up to $\nside=16$ to confirm that the posteriors are sufficiently robust against our choice of downgrade resolution beyond $\nside=2$.
\abby{TODO: add this in appendix?}

The distance metric is the Euclidean distance between the mock map and the observed data map,
\begin{equation}
    \delta_i = \sqrt{\vec d_\mathrm{sim}(\vec\theta_i)-\vec d_\mathrm{data}}
\end{equation}
where each $\vec d$ is a $\nside$-vector of the quasar overdensity in each healpixel, and $\vec\theta_i=\{\dipamp\}$ are the parameters used to generate mock $i$.
The mock is accepted if $\delta_i$ is below some acceptance threshold $\varepsilon$, and rejected otherwise.

The main hyperparameters in the ABC procedure are the number of accepted samples per generation and the maximum number of generations.
A calibration run determines the initial acceptance threshold $\varepsilon$.
Each generation, the accepted samples from the previous generation are perturbed via importance sampling, and the posterior probability distributions and acceptance thresholds are updated.
A generation runs until the desired number of accepted samples have been generated.
The final posterior probability distribution is the weighted distribution of parameters used to generate the final generation of accepted mocks.

\subsection{Likelihood-ratio approach}
\label{sec:likelihood_approach}

One way to read the \catwise dipole claim of S21 is as a classic hypothesis test between two ``nested'' hypotheses.
The first of these nested hypotheses is $H0$ (the null), which is that the dipole in the quasar sample matches the kinematic expectation from the CMB.
The second of these is $H1$, which is that the dipole has a larger amplitude by a factor of $q$ than the kinematic expectation but the same direction.
These hypotheses are nested in that $H0$ makes identical predictions to $H1$ when $q=1$.

When there are two nested hypotheses, the ``uniformly most powerful test'' in frequentist statistics is the simple likelihood ratio test.
The likelihood is defined to be the probability density (pdf) for the data given the hypothesis, evaluated at the observed data.
The likelihood ratio test not only can be used to choose between the hypotheses but also delivers a quantitative frequentist confidence level (in terms of a p-value or a number-of-sigmas level).
Any other method---any method other than the likelihood ratio---for determining the p-value is less powerful, and not justifiable if it delivers a \emph{smaller} p-value (greater number-of-sigmas level) than the likelihood-ratio-based p-value.

Write-up from Kate (from meeting with Kate and Abby 2025-04-15):
\begin{itemize}
    \item The amplitude of shot noise translated to excess power is not the most relevant quantity. Rather, it is whether the excess power level can explain the high dipole amplitude; aka translating excess power to dipole units, to see if they are consistent.
    \item However, the excess power level is just a simple model; the data may not (do not) have an exactly flat spectrum. the fit weights all ells and ends up tending towards the mean, so individual multipoles may well have high excess power (above the inferred excess power level, and/or above shot noise). This means that even if we find low excess power overall, there might be a dipole or other fluctuation that would cause us to measure a high dipole amplitude.
    \item If we find high excess power, it should mean that at least some of the modes are relatively high. These may not necessarily affect the dipole amplitude as they could either constructively or destructively interfere with the kinematic dipole, so we cannot make clear claims about the dipole amplitude from the excess power estimate.
    \item Overall, we cannot make a clear statement about whether the high dipole amplitude we find is a result of excess power, based on our posteriors.
    \item To have some interpretation of the excess power level, we should be able to compare the shot noise level to the excess power. We can estimate the shot noise by running many realizations of the map with the known base rate, and then compute the C\_ells. These C\_ells should be in the same units as the excess power used as a parameter.
    \item Note that this shot noise procedure is a bit different than the plot we looked at applying the inference pipeline to a shot noise realization, as the posteriors will be targeted towards that particular realization. We should estimate the shot noise just from an average of maps, not using any inference, I believe.
\end{itemize}
